Stop Seeking Approval From Everyone

The goal is not to stop caring what people think. It is to stop making their reaction the final authority on your choices.

Approval feels safe because it gives immediate social feedback. Someone agrees, praises you, likes the decision, or tells you that you are doing the right thing, and uncertainty becomes quieter. The problem begins when that relief becomes necessary.

When you depend on approval, ordinary decisions become negotiations. What should I wear? Should I post this? Is it okay to say no? Will they be disappointed? Did they misunderstand me? You can spend so much energy predicting reactions that you stop asking the more important question: What do I actually believe is right for me?

Approval becomes expensive

Constantly checking other people's reactions fragments your attention. You start editing yourself before anyone has even asked. You soften every opinion, explain every boundary, and delay choices until someone else makes you feel certain.

The cost is not simply indecision. Over time, excessive approval-seeking weakens your relationship with your own judgment. If every decision needs outside confirmation, your internal standard never gets enough practice.

“You can listen to feedback without surrendering ownership of the decision.”

Approval is not the same as respect

A mature person can value other people's opinions without treating them as commands. Friends, family, colleagues, and partners can offer information you cannot see. Listening is useful. Surrendering your judgment is different.

Sometimes people will disagree with a reasonable choice. That does not automatically mean the choice was wrong. Other people's discomfort can be information, but it is not always a verdict.

Build an internal standard

Before asking whether people will approve, ask a few questions that return the decision to you:

  • Is this honest?
  • Does this match my values?
  • Is it useful or necessary?
  • Am I choosing this because I want it, or because I fear a reaction?
  • Will I respect this decision tomorrow?

These questions do not eliminate uncertainty. They give uncertainty a place inside a larger framework. You can be unsure and still decide.

Start with small choices

You do not need to become radically independent overnight. Confidence grows through repeated evidence. Make small decisions and allow yourself to own them.

  • Choose a meal without polling everyone.
  • Say no once without inventing a complicated excuse.
  • Wear or use something you genuinely like without checking whether everyone approves.
  • Share a useful idea without monitoring every reaction.
  • Make one reasonable decision each day that you are willing to own completely.

After each decision, resist the urge to immediately ask whether everyone liked it. Give yourself time to experience the result. You are teaching yourself that uncertainty can be survived and that a reasonable decision does not need unanimous approval.

Learn the difference between feedback and permission

Ask for feedback when another person's expertise or perspective can improve the decision. Do not ask for permission when you have already made a reasonable choice and are only looking for relief from anxiety.

That distinction is subtle but powerful. Feedback helps you think better. Approval-seeking often asks someone else to carry the emotional responsibility for your decision.

The confident response

When you notice yourself chasing approval, pause before sending the extra message, asking the fifth person, or rewriting the decision to make it more acceptable. Ask what you already know.

Confidence is not the absence of other people's opinions. It is the ability to hear those opinions, consider them honestly, and still remain the owner of your choices.
