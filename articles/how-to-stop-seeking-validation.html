How to Stop Seeking Validation From Others: 7 Ways to Trust Yourself More

You do not need to stop caring what people think. You need to stop making other people's approval the foundation of your self-worth, your decisions, or your peace of mind.

Maybe you send a message and immediately check whether the other person has replied. Maybe you share something online and keep refreshing to see the reaction. Maybe you make a decision you believe is right, then ask three people whether they think it was the right choice.

Those habits can look harmless because seeking feedback is a normal part of life. The problem starts when reassurance stops being useful information and becomes something you need in order to feel okay.

External validation is approval, praise, agreement, attention, or reassurance coming from outside you. Self-validation is the ability to recognize your own feelings, values, effort, and judgment without requiring someone else to sign off on them.

Current writing on validation seeking repeatedly emphasizes the same distinction: the goal is not indifference to other people. It is moving from dependence on approval toward a steadier internal standard. “Approval can be useful information. It becomes a problem when you need it before you can trust yourself.”

Validation is normal. Dependence is different.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying a compliment, asking a trusted friend for perspective, or wanting reassurance during a difficult moment.

The important question is what happens when approval is missing.

  • Can you still feel good about a reasonable decision?
  • Can you tolerate someone disagreeing with you?
  • Can you leave a message unanswered without inventing a negative story?
  • Can you act on a preference without checking whether everyone agrees?

When the answer is consistently no, your attention may be organized around other people's reactions rather than your own standards.

Why seeking validation feels so powerful

Approval temporarily reduces uncertainty. When someone says, “You're right,” “That looks great,” or “I understand,” the internal tension drops.

That relief can teach you to repeat the behavior. You feel uncertain, seek reassurance, feel better for a moment, then become uncertain again the next time a decision or social situation appears.

Over time, the habit can become automatic: uncertainty → ask → reassurance → relief → uncertainty again.

The practical problem is that you never give your own judgment enough opportunity to become familiar and reliable.

1. Notice what you are actually asking for

Start by catching the moment when you reach outward.

Ask yourself:

  • Am I asking for information?
  • Am I asking for perspective?
  • Or am I asking someone to remove the discomfort of uncertainty?

Those are different needs.

“Which option has the stronger evidence?” is a useful question.

“Tell me I didn't make a mistake” may be a request for emotional certainty.

You do not need to shame yourself for the second one. Simply noticing it gives you a choice.

2. Pause before checking, asking, or posting

Validation-seeking is often driven by speed. You feel uncertainty and immediately reach for a phone, a friend, a partner, a comment section, or another opinion.

Create a small gap.

Try saying:

  • “I can wait ten minutes.”
  • “I do not need an answer right now.”
  • “Let me decide what I think first.”
  • “I can handle not knowing yet.”

That pause matters because it separates the feeling of uncertainty from the action of seeking reassurance. 3. Build a personal standard before asking for outside opinions

Many people ask for opinions before they have decided what they value.

Reverse the order.

Before asking someone else what you should do, write down your own standard:

  • What matters most here?
  • What choice best matches my values?
  • What do I already know?
  • What trade-off am I willing to accept?
  • What would I respect myself for doing?

Then ask for feedback only when another person's perspective could genuinely improve the decision.

This is the difference between consulting someone and outsourcing your judgment.

4. Practice self-validation instead of self-approval

Self-validation does not mean telling yourself that every choice was perfect.

It means acknowledging your experience honestly.

You can say:

  • “I understand why this was difficult for me.”
  • “I was nervous, and I still handled it.”
  • “I made the best decision I could with the information I had.”
  • “I can learn from this without attacking myself.”
  • “Someone disagreeing with me does not erase my perspective.”

This is more stable than trying to convince yourself that you are always right. A strong internal standard leaves room for correction without turning every mistake into a verdict about your worth.

5. Stop using other people's reactions as a mirror

Validation-seeking often creates constant self-monitoring.

You watch their expression. You study the tone of a message. You notice how quickly they reply. You compare the number of likes. You interpret silence.

Then you use those signals to decide how you feel about yourself.

The problem is that other people's reactions are noisy. They are influenced by their mood, priorities, attention, personality, circumstances, and experiences that have nothing to do with your value.

A quiet response does not automatically mean you did something wrong. A strong positive response does not automatically mean the decision was wise.

Use reactions as data when appropriate. Do not use them as a permanent mirror.

6. Train yourself to survive disagreement

One of the fastest ways to become less dependent on validation is to stop treating disagreement as an emergency.

Someone can disagree with your opinion.

Someone can dislike your boundary.

Someone can prefer a different decision.

You can still remain steady.

Try responding with:

  • “I understand your perspective.”
  • “I see it differently.”
  • “That makes sense from your point of view.”
  • “I've thought about it, and this is the choice I'm making.”

Confidence does not require unanimous agreement. It requires enough internal stability to consider disagreement without immediately abandoning your position.

7. Make small decisions without asking permission

Self-trust is built through evidence.

Pick ordinary decisions and own them completely.

  • Choose where you want to eat.
  • Wear what you actually like.
  • Publish the idea you believe is useful.
  • Decline a request that does not fit your priorities.
  • Choose between reasonable options without polling everyone.
  • Let a decision stand for a while before asking whether people liked it.

Social media can make validation-seeking louder

Social platforms make other people's reactions unusually visible. A post can be measured in likes, comments, views, shares, replies, and follower counts.

That visibility can turn normal social feedback into constant self-monitoring.

Instead of asking, “Was this worth sharing?” you may start asking, “Did people like it enough?”

Instead of asking, “Do I actually believe this?” you may ask, “Will this be received well?”

You do not have to abandon social media to change that pattern. A useful first step is to create moments where you make or share something without immediately checking the reaction.

Validation and relationships

Romantic relationships can be especially difficult because emotional closeness naturally increases the importance of another person's response.

You may notice yourself asking:

  • “Do they still like me?”
  • “Why did they take so long to reply?”
  • “Are they upset with me?”
  • “Was that message too much?”
  • “Are we okay?”

Sometimes these questions point to something that genuinely needs communication. Other times, they are attempts to eliminate uncertainty that cannot be eliminated by one more conversation.

A stronger approach is to communicate directly when communication is needed, then allow the other person to have their own reaction.

You do not need to monitor every emotional change in someone else to maintain a healthy relationship.

What to do when you feel the urge for reassurance

Use this four-step reset:

  1. Name it: “I want reassurance right now.”
  2. Identify the fear: “I'm afraid they will think badly of me.”
  3. Check the evidence: “What do I actually know?”
  4. Choose deliberately: “Do I need information, or can I tolerate the uncertainty?”

Sometimes you will still ask. That is fine. The goal is not to become completely independent of other people. The goal is to become intentional about when and why you seek their input.

A 7-day self-validation challenge

For one week, choose one moment each day to delay external validation.

Day 1: Make one small decision without asking anyone.

Day 2: Send one message without repeatedly checking for a response.

Day 3: Express one honest preference without softening it to make everyone comfortable.

Day 4: Receive a compliment without immediately dismissing it or asking whether the person really meant it.

Day 5: Let someone disagree with you without trying to win back their approval.

Day 6: Do something you enjoy without measuring how other people react to it.

Day 7: Write down three decisions from the week that you handled without needing permission.

Look at the pattern. The goal is evidence, not perfection.

What changes when approval becomes a bonus

When you stop depending on validation, you do not stop appreciating people.

You can still enjoy praise.

You can still ask for advice.

You can still care deeply about relationships.

The difference is that approval becomes a bonus rather than a requirement.

You can hear, “I don't agree,” without immediately thinking, “Then I must be wrong.”

You can hear, “I don't like that,” without automatically thinking, “Then I should change.”

You can hear silence without filling every blank with a negative story.

That is a quieter kind of confidence.

When professional support may help

Seeking reassurance occasionally is normal. It may be worth talking with a qualified mental health professional when validation-seeking is persistent, difficult to control, or strongly affecting relationships, work, sleep, daily functioning, or your sense of worth. People-pleasing and external validation can overlap with broader emotional or anxiety-related patterns, and professional support can help you understand what is maintaining the cycle.

This article is educational and is not a diagnosis or a substitute for professional mental health care.

The confident way forward

You do not have to become someone who never cares what anyone thinks.

You are building something more realistic:

Listen without surrendering. Learn without shrinking. Receive approval without needing it.

Your worth does not become more real because someone confirms it.

Your judgment does not become more valid because everyone agrees.

And your confidence does not need to be loud. It only needs to become strong enough that you can remain connected to yourself when the outside world is uncertain.
